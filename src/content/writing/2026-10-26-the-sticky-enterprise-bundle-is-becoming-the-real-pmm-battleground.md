Anthropic’s filing points to a subtle but important shift in enterprise AI buying: the winner may not be the model that gets adopted first, but the vendor that can make the initial purchase feel incomplete without a second product. According to TechCrunch’s reading of the S-1, more than half of Anthropic’s $100,000-plus customers now use both the Claude Platform and a first-party app, a pattern the story compares to Amazon-style cross-sell behavior ([source](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/26/anthropics-ipo-filing-reveals-its-strategy-for-keeping-enterprise-customers-hooked/)). My read is that this is not just a revenue tactic; it is a positioning lesson. In enterprise AI, product marketing is increasingly responsible for turning “one good use case” into a connected operating system of use cases.

### The real moat is adjacency, not just adoption

PMMs often describe expansion as a downstream outcome of product value. That is still true, but it is no longer specific enough. In AI, where switching costs can be low at the task layer and competition is often model-to-model, the more defensible motion is adjacency: adding a second product that changes the customer’s workflow, governance posture, or internal distribution so the first product becomes harder to replace. Anthropic’s customer pattern suggests that the best enterprise PMMs should stop thinking only in terms of seat expansion and start thinking in terms of workflow capture.

That distinction matters because a single-use-case AI purchase is fragile. It can be benchmarked, budgeted, and challenged by procurement as a point solution. But when the same buyer also uses a first-party app, the conversation shifts. Now the vendor is not merely supplying intelligence; it is supplying multiple touchpoints across the job-to-be-done, which raises the cost of fragmentation. The cross-sell is doing strategic work before it is doing financial work.

### A diagnostic for PMMs: are you selling a feature or a system?

For product marketers, the practical question is whether the second product feels like an upsell or an extension. I use three questions to pressure-test that:

1. **Does the second product deepen the original workflow, or just add another billing line?** If the answer is the latter, the motion will be vulnerable to discounting.
2. **Does it create a new stakeholder in the account?** If your bundle only serves the same user, you are likely compressing ACV without increasing resilience.
3. **Does it change the buying narrative from “try this tool” to “standardize on this platform”?** That is the point where positioning begins to protect retention.

If PMMs cannot answer yes to at least two of those, the expansion story is probably being carried by sales motion rather than product design. That is expensive, and it usually does not scale cleanly.

### What this changes in messaging

The implication for messaging is that feature narratives should be organized around mutual dependence, not product isolation. If the platform and app are genuinely stronger together, the customer story should show that the app creates demand, the platform structures it, and the combination reduces operational drag. The language should reflect a system of work, not a catalog of capabilities.

That also changes enablement. Sales teams need more than a demo path; they need account-level narrative maps that explain why the customer’s second purchase is an operating decision, not a convenience purchase. Customer marketing should reinforce that same logic with usage patterns, governance wins, and examples of cross-functional adoption.

The non-obvious lesson here is that in enterprise AI, retention strategy is becoming a packaging strategy. PMMs who can articulate the logic of adjacency will do more than support expansion; they will help shape the product architecture the market is willing to pay for.
