## The new buying motion starts with a settings screen

The most interesting signal in this packet is not that Google changed its privacy defaults, or that Microsoft cut more jobs, or even that AI accelerators keep multiplying. The more useful implication is that AI products are increasingly entering companies through a trust decision before they ever become a usage decision. When a product can absorb customer behavior into model training by default, the buyer is no longer only evaluating capability. They are also evaluating whether the product creates a durable source of value or an invisible leak of data, labor, and control. That changes how PMMs should position AI tools, especially in B2B.

The factual backdrop matters. TechCrunch reported that a Google privacy setting change now lets the company train its AI on more user data unless people opt out ([source](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/06/if-you-use-google-youre-training-its-ai-heres-how-to-opt-out/)). Amazon is also stopping new Mechanical Turk customers, which is another sign that the old model of cheap, distributed human labor is becoming harder to scale ([source](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/05/amazon-will-stop-accepting-new-customers-for-mechanical-turk/)). Together, those shifts suggest a market where both data and labor are being pulled tighter into platform control.

## What this means for PMM: control is part of the product

The non-obvious implication is that “privacy,” “governance,” and “human-in-the-loop” can no longer sit in the trust center as compliance language. For AI products, they are increasingly part of the value proposition itself. A buyer does not just want a model that performs well; they want to know where their prompts, outputs, and edge cases go, who can learn from them, and what operational work disappears versus migrates into a new hidden queue.

That matters because the strongest AI messaging I’m seeing across the market often overweights output quality and underweights control boundaries. But in procurement, the question is rarely “Is this smarter than the alternative?” It is closer to, “What am I implicitly training if I deploy this broadly, and what happens if I need to reverse course later?” If you cannot answer that cleanly, product usage may still happen, but expansion slows because security, legal, and operations teams become the real evaluators.

## A useful PMM diagnostic

I think a good field test for AI positioning is to ask four questions:

1. **What data does the product learn from by default?** If the answer is hard to explain, the buyer will assume the worst.
2. **What can the customer turn off without degrading core value?** This reveals whether control is real or ornamental.
3. **Where does human labor move after automation?** If work simply shifts into review, exception handling, or prompt maintenance, say so plainly.
4. **Who in the buying group feels the benefit first, and who feels the risk first?** If those are different people, the launch narrative needs two layers: adoption value and governance assurance.

These questions are useful because they move PMM away from generic “secure and compliant” claims and toward concrete tradeoffs a buyer can evaluate. They also expose where a product has a packaging opportunity. A vendor that can articulate clear training boundaries, admin controls, or customer-owned data lanes is not just checking a box; it is reducing the friction that slows enterprise rollout.

## How to message this without sounding defensive

The mistake is to frame control as a concession: here are the knobs if you’re worried. The better move is to position control as what makes broad use economically viable. In AI, trust settings are now part of the cost of adoption, the same way reliability and integrations are. If a product helps a company ship faster but leaves leaders unclear about what gets learned, retained, or exposed, it creates a second-order adoption tax that shows up later in review cycles, redlines, and stalled expansion.

For PMMs, that means the job is not only to prove the model is good. It is to make the customer feel that using it broadly will not quietly create a new dependency they cannot govern. The next AI category winner may be the one that makes that tradeoff legible before the first pilot becomes a policy problem.
